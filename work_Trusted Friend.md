#### Trusted Friends
"Deep, connected relationships are built on authenticity in both word and deed. In order to be a trusted friend, we step into the joy and sorrow of family with empathy, honesty about our own brokenness, and confidence that Jesus can meet every need."

Most modern English translations of the gospels don't make much use of the words "authenticity", "transparency" or "vulnerability." Instead, they talk about "living in the light" and "living according to the truth." There is plenty to unpack in those phrases, and while they often mean *more*, they certainly don't mean *less* than "being transparent" or "being real" with others.

In fact, you could say that transparency is a prerequisite for many of Scripture's exhortations. How are we to serve on another (Gal 5.13), comfort one another (2 Cor 13.11), forgive one another (Eph 4.32), encourage one another (1 Th 4.18), pray for one another (Jas 5.16),  bear one another's burdens (Gal 6.2) unless we know one another in our neediness and brokenness?

Being honest about our own brokenness is a prophetic act in the age of social media. It's the very opposite of a carefully curated Instagram account that posts only the highlights. It's an acknowledgment of the truth about the world and ourselves.

Being a trusted friend means honestly sharing our own lives, and also being a safe person with whom others can honestly share their lives. It means being someone who rejoices with those who rejoice, weeps with those who weep, and trusts that, no matter what, there is hope with Jesus.

Within your role at FamilyLife, what opportunities might you have to practice honesty about your brokenness?

What might help you become a safer person for others to share their lives with?

### Trusted Friend

In 2023 the US Surgeon General released an advisory on a new (or newly acknowledged) epidemic: loneliness. The human need for deep connection is not new, but the changing circumstances in our world and culture have, in many ways, made this connection more difficult. The medicine we need is friendship.

We at FamilyLife are committed to living out the great commandment to love our neighbor by being trusted friends.




---

We step into the joy and sorrow of family with empathy, honesty about our own brokenness, and confidence that Jesus can meet every need.





**We step into the joy and sorrow of family with...**

**Empathy**

The Apostle Paul gives an interesting admonition to the church in Rome. After 11 dense chapters of theological wrestling, in the twelfth chapter Paul turns to application. He appeals to the believers in Rome to present their bodies as living sacrifices, and he proceeds to expound upon what exactly that means.

In this explanation we see many of the encouragements that we might expect to see: Love, Serve, Pray, Bless, etc. But then we also see an encouragement that, to many of us, may seem a little less obvious: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep."

This is a call to treat one another with empathy - to seek to truly listen, and to understand and share the feelings of another.

Typically, it is easier for us to rejoice with others than to weep with others (there are exceptions: for instance, someone struggling with infertility may have trouble rejoicing with a sister who is pregnant). This makes sense. The emotions which accompany rejoicing are more comfortable than the ones that accompany weeping. But God calls us to be open even to the uncomfortable emotions as we seek to stand in solidarity with our neighbors.

That means responding not with platitudes, or shallow, detached encouragement, but by taking seriously the pain of another.

“You’re not alone” is an oft-repeated message of comfort to those going through a difficult time. To show empathy to a neighbor is to deliver on this promise.

On the other hand, when we engage our neighbor without empathy, we miss occasions to glorify God: failure to rejoice with those who rejoice is a missed opportunity to praise God, and failure to acknowledge the sorrow and difficulty of another downplays the coming redemption.


**Honesty about our own brokenness**

Most modern English translations of the gospels don't make much use of the words "honesty", "transparency" or "vulnerability." Instead, they talk about "living in the light" and "living according to the truth." There is plenty to unpack in those phrases, and while they often mean *more*, they certainly don't mean *less* than "being transparent" or "being real" with others.

In fact, you could say that transparency is a prerequisite for many of Scripture's exhortations. How are we to serve on another (Gal 5.13), comfort one another (2 Cor 13.11), forgive one another (Eph 4.32), encourage one another (1 Th 4.18), pray for one another (Jas 5.16),  bear one another's burdens (Gal 6.2) unless we know one another in our neediness and brokenness?

Being honest about our own brokenness is a prophetic act in the age of social media. It's the very opposite of a carefully curated Instagram account that posts only the highlights. It's an acknowledgment of the truth about the world and ourselves.

However, this is often a struggle for many of us. We want others to think well of us, and so we may shy away from letting others see our brokenness, thinking that will lower their opinion of us. And we may also worry that exposing our brokenness will lower others’ opinion of God - that they would see Him as unable or unwilling to fix us.

There is risk involved in being honest about our own brokenness, but there is also opportunity.

Being honest with others about our brokenness is an invitation for others to show us empathy. And that invitation is a gift because God created them to experience the joy of love and service too! Trusted friendship is a two-way street. We are not always in the position of the teacher, the giver, the helper; sometimes we must also be the learner, the receiver, the helped.

Revealing ourselves to be broken also protects against a common distortion of the Bible’s teaching: the prosperity gospel. The Good News is not that God will make our lives comfortable and take away all our struggles, it’s that He is with us and gives us hope!

Letting others see our brokenness also serves as an expression of solidarity because brokenness is something we all have in common.

**Confidence that Jesus can meet every need**

This is the hope of the gospel. While having *our* friendship is wonderful, our friends need the friendship of Jesus much more. God may use us to meet some needs, but Jesus is the one who can meet every need.

We want to leave space for uncomfortable emotions, but we also want to - at the appropriate time - put before our friends the hope that is in Christ (whether for the first time or the thousandth time).

God loves to meet our needs. Jesus devotes a significant chunk of His most famous sermon to that very fact.

Matt 6.26-7.11

"Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they?" 

...

"Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow: they neither toil nor spin, yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is alive and tomorrow is thrown into the oven, will he not much more clothe you[?]" 

...

"If you then, who are evil, know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!"

In addition to meeting our needs for things like food and clothing, God also can meet our needs for healing. God loves to bring healing to those who are suffering. Jesus mentioned healing in the announcement of his ministry (Lk 4.16-21), and as we read the gospel accounts, we can see that his ministry was defined as much by his healing as it was by his teaching.

Jesus' brother, writing decades later, continues to encourage God's children to look to Him for healing:

Jas 5.13-14

"Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise. Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the church, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord."

Jesus *does* meet our needs, and yet sometimes there will be areas of our lives where we do not see the types of changes we hope for (for example, Paul's thorn in the flesh - 2 Cor 12.17). This can be confusing and disorienting, but we can trust that God is seeing the forest while we only see the trees. And even in these areas, we can look forward to the day when all will be made well - when all our tears are wiped away, and when "everything sad has come untrue." (Jesus Storybook Bible)

---

In in our empathy, our honesty about our own brokenness, and our confidence that Jesus can meet every need, we pursue a kind of togetherness. We want to be together with others in their stuff, we want to invite others to be together with us in our stuff, and we want to remember that Jesus is together with us through it all. To pursue this type of togetherness is to be a trusted friend.
