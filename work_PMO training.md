			- Help set team members up for success so that they are able to make their contribution, and so that their unique contributions serve the same whole. Be the connective tissue of project teams.
				- Imagine hitting a nail with a hammer. You need muscle to initiate movement of the hammer towards the nail. You also need bone to provide stiffness and direction. Without either muscle or bone, you wouldn't be able to hammer in the nail (you'd either have an explosion of undirected energy, or no movement at all). But even when you have both muscle and bone, they are only able to work together when they are connected by tendons and ligaments. That connective tissue organizes the muscle and bone so that their unique properties and abilities contribute to the same action. Project managers are like that connective tissue. We help to organize the members of a team so that their unique contributions serve the same whole.
				- At the meta-level, the PMO can be like the connective tissue for the organization.
					- This is certainly true and needed within a given cross-departmental project, but it can be true (and is needed) beyond the scope of individual projects.
					- Because we touch so many parts of the organization, we have the opportunity to become aware of the strengths/weaknesses/capacities/needs of different parts of the organization, and may sometimes be able to discern spots where different teams could work together in mutually beneficial ways. (And also potentially find places where duplicate work is being done).
				Another part of helping teammates make their best contribution is ensuring that they are free to make that contribution, and not blocked by lots of other things. Removing roadblocks could mean a lot of different things: obtaining important information, resources, tools, etc. to enable teammates to do their part, learning how to fulfill small roles that don’t naturally fall to anyone else (filling gaps), getting permissions/approvals, organizing logistics (setting/facilitating meetings, etc.)
			Both of these aspects are service-oriented. We seek to help others thrive. We use our power to empower our teammates. (Mark 10, 1 Cor 12, Matt 3)

			Before - Prepare:
				As much as possible, try to understand the conversations that need to be had, who needs to speak into them, and be prepared to frame the conversations in such a way that everyone understands the issue and what resolution should look like (where we are and where we want to go).
					Ex. The hotel doesn’t have enough rooms for us for staff conference… (what would be helpful to frame this up to be a productive conversation?)
					There may be someone in the meeting who can frame the conversation better than you (they may be more familiar with the details). Don’t be shy about asking them to do so.
					Sometimes, especially in a complex conversation, it can be helpful to try and boil the conversation down into a single question. That way, everyone knows when the conversation is finished/if it has been successful—when the question has been answered.
			During - Track the flow of conversation:
				Sometimes conversations can take a lot of turns. Depending on the importance/urgency of the intended conversation, and the importance/urgency of the unintended conversation, you may or may not want to try and gracefully steer the conversation back to the original topic/question. This often takes some familiarity with the content/situations being discussed. Trust your intuition.
				Try and guide the conversations in such a way that the team is able to adequately discuss all the things that absolutely need to be discussed at a given meeting (sometimes it just may not be possible, and an extra meeting needs to be scheduled)
			During - Verbalize (and probably document) next steps
				Make sure everyone is clear about next steps coming out of each conversation. For some conversations, that will just mean teammate A, B and C doing X, Y and Z to implement the decision(s) that were made. For other conversations, a next step might be a certain teammate gathering a couple pieces of essential information so that the conversation can continue next week. Wherever the conversation ended, make sure that everyone (and especially the responsible people) understand what needs to happen next in order to keep moving toward resolution.
				Sometimes conversations can just fizzle out, and it’s helpful to just directly ask the question: Ok, what’s the best next step here?
			After - Documentation:
				Depending on the meeting, it can be helpful to send out notes after the fact (so that what was discussed verbally is also available in writing). Seems like a lot of the time AI note taking can be relied on for this. In meetings with lots of important next steps, it could be good to proof the AI notes, or just record them yourself.

If there's a roadblock that needs to be cleared and no PM, the roadblock still needs to be cleared. Same thing for connectivity. If there is no PM, everyone else just pitches in, and they are unable to give their full energy to their primary role. And typically the work suffers. But when there is a PM who is managing well, everyone else can give their full attention to the things they are gifted and trained to do, and their work is more enjoyable, and they are platformed to succeed.

### Project management at its simplest

At its absolutely most simple: step1: make a (good) list, step2: look at the list

Now, it's a little more complicated than that right? You have to make sure the right stuff makes it onto that list, and you have to make sure that the right people are paying attention to that list at the right time. But I promise, it's not rocket science.

If you have a list and some sort of structure in place to make sure that you (and your teammates) see the list at the right time, then you have a trusted system. And that's a reassuring thing to have!

### Example: Trip to the grocery store

Ok, let's imagine a simple project that we all have experience with: a grocery store trip

Someone describe a very unsuccessful trip: I don't see anything at home that looks good to eat -- so, I go straight to the store and get a box of fried chicken; or, I need some lemon juice to make dinner and notice that we are out, so I go to the store, get lemon juice, and come home. Upon returning home, I realize we are also out of three other things I need to make dinner.

What would make this trip better?

- **Seek input** from the necessary people
	- who's making dinner tonight?
	- what do the next couple days look like?
- **Make a list**
	- *what's the scope?* 
		- are we just replacing staples? 
		- are we shopping for a more elaborate meal for when friends come over on Saturday? 
		- meal planning for the week?
	- Making a list is going to help us get the things we need, and avoid getting the things we don't need
- **Determine timing**
	- I need to wait to go until a prescription is ready
	- but I also need to go by 2pm so I have time to marinate the chicken
- **Assign tasks**
	- In this example, these might all fall to one person unless you need you need things from multiple stores
- **Do the work**: Go to the store, at the right time, and BRING THE LIST!

### RACI chart

**Responsible** = the person (or people) who does the work

**Accountable** = the person (only one) who ensures the work is done according to success criteria

**Consulted** = the people whose input is solicited *before* a decision is made

**Informed** = the people who are informed *after* a decision is made


### When to use a charter?

The higher the stakes, and the more opportunity for confusion or miscommunication, the more likely you will want to use a project charter.

You can think of it as an alignment tool; a record of agreement that you can revisit if difficult decisions need to be made.

If it's just you and a teammate doing something simple, probably no need. But if multiple teams are working together on something important, complex and expensive, a charter is a good idea.

### Documentation

- Find the balance
	- Documentation isn't free. It costs time and energy (which could be used for something else).
	- Documentation of some things is very worth the time and effort, and will save future time/effort/stress/money/etc.
	- However, if we spend hours and hours documenting something and then never refer to it, then that ends up being wasted time/energy.
	- Choosing what level of documentation to aim for is a risk management question. What is the likelihood that this information will be needed in the future (and won't be available elsewhere), and is it worth the time/effort required to accommodate for that likelihood?
		- In FL, it doesn't make much sense to pour tons of resources into earthquake preparation, but it may make sense to pour a lot of resources into hurricane preparation.
		- Choosing the extent to which we document stuff is kind of like that.
	- Our Resources folder is an example of a space where we can make a documentation decision. Most folders from the past few years are in decent shape. Many of the older files are organized in a pretty unusable way, and some are incomplete. We could go back and reorganize those folders, and search through flpprojects@familylife.com to find missing files. But that would be a pretty big lift, and we are only asked for older files every few months. It may be better to just let the old files be messy and address each individually when the need arises.
