    - Invest in your community of faith

    - Does not expect her husband to satisfy all her needs

    - Respect is:
        - Cultivating a heart attitude of unconditional respect
        - Noticing and affirming where he's winning
        - Showing appreciation for his desire to provide for you and your family
        - Affirming his masculinity by responding to an initiating sexual intimacy
    - Respect in not:
        - Excusing sinful behavior
        - Criticizing or correcting your husband in public
        - Feeling you can't disagree or have differing opinions with him
        - Rejecting him because of his weaknesses
- Love your husband by supporting him
- Submission does not mean:
    - You are inferior or lose your identity
    - You blindly obey or submit to verbal, emotional, spiritual, or physical mistreatment
    - You give input, but your husband makes all the decisions
    - You follow your husband into sin

    - Giving up your desire to control
    - Viewing support as a complementary and not competitive way of relating to your husband
    - Cooperating with your husband as he seeks to lead your marriage and family
- Love your husband by working together to build a God-centered family
    - Start with you: Be what you want your kids to become
    - Love your spouse: A strong marriage provides security for your kids
    - Be a team: Work together to create an environment for God to shape their hearts

### Dancing analogy

Imagine leadership and deference like a dance.

It takes two to tango, right? (or whatever kind of couples dance)... When two people are trying to do a dance together, one may be a bit more practiced than the other. Imagine the guy is doing his best, but he's mis-stepping, stepping on toes, etc. How should his partner respond? Quit and walk away, assuming he'll never get it? No, keep dancing with him. She could opt to hold him forcefully and jerk him into all the right positions at all the right times, but that wouldn't be fun for anyone. Instead she could continue to dance her part, gently guiding him into the right movements until he can dance his own part without so many cues.

What if he's not really doing his best to do the dance together? How should the partner respond? This is when it gets a little trickier. What if mid-song, he checks his phone? He's still kind of going through the motions, but his attentione is clearly divided. The partner may be annoyed or feel neglected. She could say, he's not even trying his best and I'm basically dancing with myself, I'm done with this. But a more graceful response might be to gently guide him to put the phone away (that message will be there later) and back into the dance. Appeal to him with affection rather than anger; encourage rather than demand.

What if that doesn't work? Or what if he's really not even trying at all? What if he has left the dance floor? Is the partner supposed to keep doing her part of the dance looking like a fool? I don't think so. She may leave the dance floor too so they can talk about what's going on. They may get others involved in the conversation who may be able to help them get back on the dance floor. But the principles of how to be a good dance partner change when one partner has disengaged from the activity at hand.
